# The Latest Work on Norse Gods — Research Notes

*Compiled 14 July 2026. A survey of recent archaeology, runology, and scholarship
on the Old Norse gods and pre-Christian Scandinavian religion.*

---

## 1. The headline find: the oldest known inscription naming Odin

The most significant recent development in the study of the Norse gods is the
identification of the **world's oldest known inscription naming Odin**, found on
a gold bracteate from the **Vindelev hoard** (unearthed near Vejle, central
Denmark, in 2020).

- Runologist **Lisbeth Imer** (National Museum of Denmark) and linguist
  **Krister Vasshus** deciphered the tiny runes (2–3 mm high, written without
  word breaks) and read the phrase **"He is Odin's man"**, referring to a
  portrait of an unknown king or chieftain possibly nicknamed *Jaga*/*Jagaz*.
- The bracteates date to roughly **AD 450–490**, and the hoard also contained
  Roman medallions of AD 335–383 reworked into jewellery.
- The result pushes firm evidence for the cult of Odin back to the **5th
  century — about 150 years earlier** than the previous oldest attestation,
  reshaping the chronology of Norse mythology's development.

## 2. Cult sites: the Ose "god house" and its Scandinavian parallels

Excavations at the **Ose farm near Ørsta** (Møre og Romsdal, western Norway)
uncovered the foundations of a late 8th-century **pagan temple ("god house")**
— the first structure of its kind identified in Norway.

- The wooden building measured about **14 m long, 8 m wide, and up to 12 m
  high**, and is thought to have hosted worship and sacrifices at the midsummer
  and midwinter solstices.
- Finds include **cooking pits and animal bones** — remains of ritual meals —
  and the interior likely held hearths for sacrificial fires and wooden figures
  of gods such as **Odin, Thor and Freyr**.
- Its layout closely matches late Iron Age cult houses at **Uppåkra** (Sweden,
  in use c. AD 200–950) and **Tissø** (Denmark), pointing to a shared,
  long-lived architectural tradition of dedicated cult buildings — a point that
  has revised the older view that Norse worship happened only outdoors or in
  chieftains' halls. Destroyed weapons deposited at such sites appear to have
  been part of the cult.

## 3. Thor's hammer amulets: the material cult of Thor

A steady stream of **Mjölnir (Thor's hammer) amulet** finds continues to
illuminate how ordinary people expressed devotion to Thor, especially during
the pagan–Christian transition (9th–11th centuries):

- **Ysby, Halland, Sweden (2022)**: a lead Thor's hammer amulet, the first of
  its kind in the region, decorated with embossed designs and possibly
  originally gilded or silvered, with a hole so it could be worn.
- **Þjórsárdalur valley, Iceland (2018)**: the first known **stone** Thor's
  hammer amulet, dating to the early settlement period.
- The 2014 find of a hammer amulet at Købelev (Denmark) carrying the runic text
  **"Hmar x is" ("this is a hammer")** settled a long-running debate about
  whether these pendants really depicted Mjölnir.
- Scholars read the amulets as identity markers — worn to signal allegiance to
  the old gods as Christianity spread through Scandinavia.

## 4. Recent scholarship and publications

- **Carolyne Larrington, *The Norse Myths That Shape the Way We Think*
  (Thames & Hudson, 2023)** — the leading recent reception study, tracing how
  the Norse gods live on in modern culture: Marvel's Thor, neo-pagan revival
  movements, environmental readings of Ragnarök, debates about masculinity, and
  the appropriation of Norse symbols by nationalist ideologies. It follows her
  standard reference *The Norse Myths: A Guide to the Gods and Heroes* (2017).
  ([Thames & Hudson](https://www.thamesandhudson.com/products/the-norse-myths-that-shape-the-way-we-think),
  [W. W. Norton](https://wwnorton.com/books/9780500251966))
- ***Old Norse Religion in Long-Term Perspectives: Origins, Changes &
  Interactions*** — the influential collected volume (available on
  [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.919497)) that frames the current
  research agenda: treating Old Norse religion as a regionally varied,
  centuries-deep tradition rather than a single canonical mythology.
- Accessible syntheses in current circulation include **Daniel McCoy's *The
  Viking Spirit*** (religion and cultural context alongside the myths) and
  **Johan Egerkrans's illustrated *Norse Gods***.
  ([Most Recommended Books](https://mostrecommendedbooks.com/lists/best-norse-mythology-books))

## 5. Where the field is heading

Threads that run through the recent work:

1. **Earlier origins.** The Vindelev bracteate anchors the Odin cult in the
   Migration Period (5th c.), well before the Viking Age, and strengthens the
   case that the mythology known from the 13th-century Eddas preserves genuinely
   old traditions.
2. **Built cult places.** Ose, Uppåkra and Tissø show formal temple
   architecture existed across Scandinavia, revising older "no temples" views.
3. **Religion as lived practice.** Amulets, ritual meals, and deposited weapons
   shift attention from mythological texts to how the gods were actually
   worshipped.
4. **Reception studies.** Larrington and others are examining how Norse myth is
   reused today — including critically, where the imagery is co-opted by
   extremist movements.
